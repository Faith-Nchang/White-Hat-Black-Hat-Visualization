Write-ups

White hat write-up

Change by country: marks and encodings. A sorted horizontal bar chart is used because the task is comparing one signed value across many named countries. Bar length from a zero baseline is the most accurate position-based encoding, and horizontal bars leave room for readable country names. A line chart would need dozens of overlapping lines and a map would encode value as color, which is harder to read. Blue means a decrease and red-orange means an increase, a colorblind-safe pair. Color is redundant with direction from the zero line and with the printed value labels, so the chart does not depend on color alone. The axes start at zero and are never truncated.

A toggle between % change and absolute change. Percent change alone exaggerates countries with small starting emissions, while absolute change alone hides fast-moving small countries. A toggle switches one chart between the two measures, so each view stays uncluttered and has a single axis, while the caption tells the reader when to switch. Countries re-sort by whichever measure is shown. Hovering a row shows the country's start and end values, both changes and its share of the cohort's net change.

Data transformation. From the raw yearly totals we calculate percent change and absolute change between the start and end year, sum a cohort total, and calculate each country's share of the net change. The cohort is limited to countries that report every year in the period, so each bar covers the same span; the countries left out are counted in the caption. Regional aggregates are removed so nothing is counted twice. A coverage grid under the chart shows which countries report which years, so the reader can see who is in the cohort, who is left out and why. The reader can also re-sort by value or name.

Titles and labels. The title is a neutral question that states the period rather than a conclusion. The subtitle gives the number of countries, the measure (national totals excluding land use) and how many fell or rose. Both axes carry units, the caption explains the colors, the percent-versus-absolute caveat and the exclusions, and the source is cited. This is the honest counterpart of the black hat chart: the same data, with the full period, a zero baseline and a neutral title.

Black hat write-up

Goal. The black hat chart says “emissions have fallen 13.6% since 2007”, using the same 61-country dataset as the honest charts. Every number is true; the distortion comes from one choice in each of the three areas: data transformation, marks and encodings, and titles and labels.

1. Selective filtering (data transformation). We show only 2007 to 2020. 2007 is the highest point in the record and 2020 is the COVID-19 lockdown low, so these years give the largest possible drop. Over the full 1990–2022 period emissions fell only about 2.4%, and by 2022 the drop from 2007 had shrunk to about 10.8% as emissions partly rebounded. Ending in 2020 also hides that the low came from a temporary shutdown, not lasting policy. The country set is identical in both versions, so only the time window changes.

2. Truncated y-axis (marks and encodings). The vertical axis does not start at zero, so a drop of under 14% fills most of the chart height and looks like a collapse. Line charts do not require a zero baseline, so this draws little suspicion. The axis labels stay accurate, so a careful reader can catch it. Direct labels on the 2007 value and the final change pull the eye to the endpoints and away from the scale.

3. Misleading title and color (titles and labels). “Emissions Have Fallen 13.6% Since 2007” is accurate, but with no end year it reads as a statement about today. The subtitle gives the country count and units but omits the end year, so 2020 appears only as the last x-axis tick. The green line signals good news, and a bold green “−13.6% since 2007” label frames the result before the reader checks the data.

Why it looks trustworthy. We kept the cues readers use to judge reliability: a full source line, a stated country count, labeled axes with units, light gridlines, hover values, a precise one-decimal figure and clean styling. No distortion is announced, and each is common in real reporting, so nothing looks like an error.

The honest version. “Review the distortions” has one switch per trick: the full 1990–2022 period, a zero baseline, and a neutral title and blue line. A live readout shows what each fix does, so the reader can see that the time window alone turns a 13.6% drop into a 2.4% change, while the axis changes how large the same drop looks. A heatmap of every possible start and end year shows that 2007–2020 is the single most flattering window of all 528, and that more than half of the windows show emissions rising.

Appendix

Data and references

Data source

OECD, Air Emissions GHG Inventories (dataset DF_AIR_GHG), from the OECD Data Explorer: data-explorer.oecd.org, DSD_AIR_GHG@DF_AIR_GHG. The OECD publishes national greenhouse gas inventories by country, year, pollutant and measure.

How we prepared the data

  • We downloaded the full dataset (649,251 rows and 30 columns). It is too large to publish with the site (178 MB), so the site loads a reduced file, data/emissions.csv.
  • We kept only the combined greenhouse gas total (all gases, in CO₂ equivalent) and three measures: the total excluding land use, the total including land use, and land use and forestry alone. Every chart on this page uses the total excluding land use. This reduced the data to 8,156 rows and 7 columns.
  • We converted values to million tonnes CO₂ equivalent (the reported value times its unit multiplier, divided by 1,000,000) and checked every kept row against the original. The largest difference was 0.0005 Mt, from rounding.
  • Five regional aggregates, such as the EU and OECD totals, are flagged and left out of every country chart and sum, so nothing is counted twice. That leaves 93 countries.
  • Missing observations stay missing. They are never filled in or drawn as zero.
  • The file covers 1985–2024, and the assignment describes 1990–2018. We use 1990–2022, and only the 61 countries that report every year in that period.

Development process

AI usage and reflection

Our approach: AI as a research assistant, design critic, and validation tool

We used Claude Code (Claude Sonnet 5.5) throughout the project for code generation, exploratory data analysis, design evaluation, and iterative refinement. However, our most meaningful use of AI went beyond generating code. We used it to explore how data can be presented honestly or misleadingly, evaluate our visualizations against the rubric, identify weaknesses in our designs, and investigate alternative approaches.

Our team retained responsibility for choosing the dataset, developing the white hat and black hat narratives, evaluating AI-generated suggestions, determining which features to implement or reject, and finalizing the visualizations and written explanations.

1. Novel use: Investigating how data selection can manipulate a narrative

One of our most significant uses of AI was exploring how the same greenhouse gas emissions dataset could support different interpretations depending on the selected time period.

Our exploratory analysis script tested how sensitive the totals were to the choice of time window and surfaced the 2007 peak and the 2020 COVID-era low. Later, when we built the cherry-picking heatmap, Claude evaluated 528 candidate windows and confirmed that 2007–2020 is the most favorable interval, with a reported emissions reduction of 13.6%. More than half of the windows show emissions rising. By comparison, the complete 1990–2022 period showed a reduction of only 2.4%.

This supports our black hat visualization: it shows how selectively presenting an accurate subset of data can create a misleading impression without falsifying the underlying values.

We also used AI to evaluate how changes in framing, titles, annotations, and visual presentation could influence interpretation. This connected computational analysis directly to the design and ethical objectives of the assignment.

2. AI-assisted design evaluation and iteration

We repeatedly asked Claude to evaluate our visualizations against individual rubric criteria rather than simply asking whether the charts looked good.

For the white hat visualization, its evaluations identified several weaknesses:

  • The second panel was not consistently sorted.
  • The visual encoding lacked a sufficiently clear legend.
  • Excluded countries were counted but not explicitly identified.
  • Important increases and decreases were not emphasized clearly enough.

We used these findings to improve the visualization through clearer legends, callouts for the largest increase and decrease, improved ordering, and a named list of excluded countries.

For the black hat visualization, Claude identified a contradiction between our written explanation and the actual page: our write-up claimed that the date range was concealed, even though it appeared in the subtitle. We revised the implementation and explanation to ensure our claims accurately described the finished visualization.

AI also helped us evaluate scope. Initially, we had three white hat visualizations and two black hat visualizations. We questioned whether we were prioritizing quantity over quality and asked Claude to evaluate our approach. Its feedback reinforced our decision to focus on fewer, more polished views rather than adding features that did not meaningfully improve the project.

3. Critical reflection: Where AI was wrong and how we responded

We did not automatically accept Claude’s outputs. Several recommendations were inaccurate, unclear, or unsuitable for our design goals.

  • Confusing Ukraine-adjusted comparison. Claude proposed and implemented a feature showing emissions totals without Ukraine, first as a toggle and later as an additional total row with a hover chart. Although analytically interesting, we found both implementations difficult to interpret. We removed the feature instead of keeping it merely because the code had already been written.
  • Inconsistent unit conversion. While auditing our reduced dataset, Claude identified inconsistent unit handling across two scripts. We consolidated the conversion into one script and added assertions to prevent similar discrepancies.
  • Interaction and narrative decisions. Claude initially generated sort controls that were too small. We revised their sizing. It also recommended placing the reveal section immediately beside the black hat chart, but we chose to position it after the written explanations to preserve the intended narrative sequence.

These cases reinforced an important lesson: AI-generated solutions still require verification, usability evaluation, and human judgment. We treated its recommendations as proposals to test rather than instructions to follow.

5. Why our approach is innovative

Our approach was innovative because we used AI not only to produce a visualization, but also to investigate the relationship between data analysis, visual design, and misleading communication.

The time-window exploration was particularly important. By computationally examining every possible interval, we showed how a technically accurate selection of data can produce a substantially different narrative from the full dataset. This let us document, with evidence, why our black hat window is a cherry-picked one.

We also used AI in an iterative process of proposing, evaluating, rejecting, and refining design ideas. Its contributions were not limited to implementation: it helped us identify inconsistencies, compare alternatives, test numerical claims, and critique usability.

At the same time, our team made the final decisions. We rejected features that reduced clarity, corrected inaccurate descriptions, and verified calculations before incorporating them.

6. What we learned

This project showed us that AI can be valuable as a computational and critical-thinking aid, but its recommendations are not inherently reliable.

We learned that effective AI use requires more than writing prompts and accepting generated solutions. It requires asking targeted questions, examining the resulting evidence, testing implementations, recognizing limitations, and making independent decisions.

Our most valuable results came from combining AI-assisted exploration with human evaluation. This helped us develop more deliberate visualizations while gaining a deeper understanding of how data selection and presentation can shape the conclusions readers draw.

Extra credit features

The assignment requires two visualizations and a write-up for each. These three features go beyond that. Each answers a question a reader of our charts would naturally ask, and each is built from the same data and code as the main charts.

1. Per-trick reveal switches with a live readout

What it does
Three independent switches (time window, vertical axis, title and color) undo one distortion at a time. A readout shows the percent change on screen, how much of the chart height the drop fills, and the tone of the framing.
Why it is innovative or advanced
A reveal is usually one before-and-after button. Here the distortions are separate variables, so the reader can run a small controlled experiment, and the readout turns each effect into a number (percent change, share of chart height) instead of only a visual impression.
Why it matters for our visualization
Our black hat chart combines three tricks, and a single before-and-after cannot show which one does the work. The switches separate them: the time window alone moves the headline figure from −13.6% to −2.4%, the axis changes only how large the same drop looks, and the title and color change only the tone. This is the argument of our black hat write-up, made testable.
How it goes beyond the requirements
The write-up explains the techniques. The switches let the reader check each claim by changing one variable and watching the numbers, including mixed settings.

2. Cherry-picking heatmap

What it does
A grid of every possible start year and end year (528 windows) is colored by the change in total emissions for our 61 countries, with the black hat chart's window outlined.
Why it is innovative or advanced
Instead of showing one alternative window, it draws the whole space of possible windows at once, so the reader sees where the chosen window sits among all the headlines the data could support. It needs a new calculation for every start and end year pair, rendered as a diverging color scale centered on zero.
Why it matters for our visualization
A black hat chart that selects a time window only works if that window is unrepresentative. The heatmap tests that directly: 2007–2020 is the most flattering of all 528 windows, and more than half of them (293) show emissions rising. So the selective filtering is shown to be cherry-picking, not just asserted, and it answers the fair question of whether another window would have told the same story.
How it goes beyond the requirements
It computes a new dataset (the change for every possible window) that none of the main charts need. It sits in the review section, so it does not tip off readers of the black hat chart.

3. Accessibility and keyboard navigation

What it does
The main chart works without a mouse. Tab to the chart, then use the arrow keys (plus Home and End) to move between countries. Each row shows a focus ring and a tooltip with its values and has a text label for screen readers. Direction is never shown by color alone: it also comes from the zero line and printed values. The palette is colorblind-safe, and transitions stop when the system requests reduced motion.
Why it is innovative or advanced
Static SVG charts are usually mouse-only. This one uses a single tab stop with arrow-key movement between rows (a roving focus model), labels every row for screen readers, shows the tooltip on keyboard focus, and respects the reduced-motion setting.
Why it matters for our visualization
The assignment defines a white hat chart as clear and easy to interpret for its intended audience, often the general population. A chart that needs a mouse or full color vision is not clear to part of that audience, and a visualization about honest communication should not exclude readers.
How it goes beyond the requirements, and its limit
Accessibility is not part of the rubric. The limit is that the heatmap is not keyboard-navigable, so it has a text summary and a list of its five most flattering windows instead.
